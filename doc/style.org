#+title: sift — the style it enforces, and why
#+author: Josep Bigorra
#+options: toc:2 num:nil
#+language: en

The form is formatted, the program is not: every choice below changes how a
Lisp file is *read*, never what it *does*.

* Why a style is enforced
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: why
:END:

A Lisp file has two readers: the compiler, and a person scanning a buffer for
the shape of the program.  A style serves the second.  The structure of a
Lisp program is visible in its parentheses and in nothing else, and a
formatter that respects that structure makes the shape legible in one
glance.

A project with two styles has none, which is why sift enforces one — in the
narrowest way available to it: it moves whitespace and delimiter characters
and reports what a human should do next.  An expression's meaning is never
rewritten, a form is never reflowed, and nothing is fixed unasked.

Three invariants underlie every rule:

1. *The tree is the truth.*  Indentation follows the nesting, not the
   indentation the author left behind.  Idempotence of formatting follows
   from this.
2. *Nothing is lost.*  A comment, a blank line and a `'` are content, not
   noise, and the formatter carries them through unchanged.
3. *A form states its meaning once.*  `when` instead of `(if x y)`, `unless`
   instead of `(if (not x) y)`, `let` instead of a paragraph-long
   expression, `:key` instead of the sixth positional argument.

* The ten choices
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: choices
:END:

** Two columns
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: two-columns
:END:

Every body indents two columns from the opening parenthesis:

#+begin_src scheme
(define (square x)
  (* x x))

(let ((a 1)
      (b 2))
  (+ a b))
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  A Lisp line begins with a form and its operator, so two columns
is the smallest step that is visibly a step and the one that leaves the most
room for the form itself.  It is the step shown in the Emacs Lisp manual and
in the examples of the Scheme standard, and the step every Lisp of the last
thirty years takes.  Four columns push a nested form off the right edge; one
column is not a step.

The exception is a form whose operator takes arguments on a line of its own:
those arguments align under the first of them, because they are a column of
arguments and not a body.

#+begin_src scheme
(list (car x)
      (cdr x))

(make-window title
             :width 10
             :height 20)
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  It is what every Lisp editor does with a call, what Emacs does
by default, and it keeps a list of arguments readable however long the
operator is: `(with-something-long ...)` does not push its arguments six
columns in, it puts them where the first one stands.  An operator alone on
its line has nothing to align under, so what follows it moves one column in
from the opener.

The same exception covers a *data list* — a binding list, a quoted
structure — whose elements line up under the first element:

#+begin_src scheme
(let ((short 1)
      (a-much-longer-name 2))
  ...)
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  Those elements are not a body; they are a column of things, and
a column is scanned more easily when it is aligned.

A *vector* is such a list even when its first element is a symbol.
`#(foo bar)` holds two symbols and is not a call to `foo`, so its elements
align under the first one like the bindings above; `#u8(1 2 3)` is the same
rule with numbers in it.  Reading a vector as a call is how a formatter comes
to align `#(a b` `     c)` under a column the author cannot account for.

A *clause* is the exception to the exception.  The clause of a `cond`,
`case`, `handler-case` or `condition-case` is a condition and a body, so its
body indents two columns even when its condition is a plain symbol:

#+begin_src scheme
(cond [else
        (fallback)])

(handler-case (run)
  (error (e)
    (report e)))
#+end_src

** One blank line between top-level forms
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: blank-lines
:END:

Forms at top level are separated by at most one blank line, and runs of
three or more inside a form are collapsed to two.  sift never *inserts* a
blank line where there was none: the author's grouping survives.

*Rationale.*  A blank line is a paragraph break.  In Lisp, a top-level form is
a paragraph, and two forms that belong together are separated by one.  Three
blank lines state what one states, at four times the cost.

** Closing delimiters trail — by default
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: community
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(define (f x)
  (if (null? x)
    '()
    (cons (car x)
          (f (cdr x)))))
#+end_src

The alternative, `--style=dangling`, puts each closer on its own line,
aligned with its opener:

#+begin_src scheme
(define (f x)
  (if (null? x)
    '()
    (cons (car x)
          (f (cdr x))
    )
  )
)
#+end_src

*Why trailing is the default.*  Because it is what every Lisp community
does, and because a trailing closer is *seen* to close something.  A line
holding only `)` carries no information except that one form ended somewhere
above, which the indentation has already stated.  Emacs Lisp code never
dangles; Common Lisp style guides say not to; Scheme code does not.  Dangling
is available because a project that has already chosen it should not have to
fight the formatter, and it is opt-in.

** Square brackets for `cond` clauses, in Scheme
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: brackets
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(cond [(< n 0) 'negative]
      [(= n 0) 'zero]
      [else 'positive])
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  A `cond` clause is not a call: it is a pair of *test* and
*body*, and the bracket marks where the pair ends without requiring the
reader to count parentheses.  Guile's own sources and the Guix code base are
written this way, and since Scheme reads `[` and `(` interchangeably, the
change is purely visual — the only kind of change a formatter may make.

sift applies it to the clause lists of `cond` (and `case`, when the project
asks for it) and to nothing else: a binding list is already aligned, and
bracketing every list would be noise.  A project that prefers parentheses
sets `brackets: parens`, or runs `--brackets=parens`; a project that keeps
whatever is written sets `brackets: preserve`.

It never applies to Emacs Lisp or Common Lisp, whatever the setting says: a
square bracket is syntax in neither dialect, so a `sift.yaml` that asks for
square brackets and a directory holding an `.el` file would otherwise produce
a file Emacs cannot read.  The setting concerns the two Schemes -- Guile and
Racket -- because the argument for it does: a Racket file brackets the clause
of a `cond`, a `case` and a `match` for the same reason a Guile file brackets
the clause of a `cond`.

** Keyword arguments line up

A call that passes keyword arguments lines them up under its first argument:

#+begin_src scheme
(make-window title
             :width 10
             :height 20)
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  The keywords become a column of names and values instead of a
staircase, and two calls can be compared by reading down the same column.  It
is what Emacs does for a call it knows, and what a reader does for a call
they do not.

** Macro indentation is a project's own rule

A macro that is not in sift's table is indented by the generic rule; a
project that knows better states the rule in its configuration:

#+begin_src yaml
indent-rules:
  - [with-my-thing, 2]
  - [deftransaction, 1]
#+end_src

A project rule takes precedence over the built-in table, so a local
convention is expressed without patching the tool.

`use-package` is the one third-party macro already in the table, because it is
the form an Emacs configuration is written in:

#+begin_src elisp
(use-package evil
  :ensure t
  :init
  (setq evil-want-integration t)
  :config
  (evil-mode 1))
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  A `use-package` form is not a call with arguments; it is a
sequence of clauses — `:ensure`, `:init`, `:config`, `:bind`, `:hook`,
`:custom` and the rest — and each clause owns what follows it.  The macro
declares this itself, with `(declare (indent defun))`, which is why Emacs
puts every keyword, and every body form under `:init` or `:config`, two
columns in from the opener.  A configuration formatted by pressing `TAB` is
the form above.

The generic rule gives a different result: the first argument — the package
name — stands on the operator's line, so everything after it aligns under the
name, thirteen columns in for `(use-package evil`.  That is what a call does
with its arguments, and it is not what a form made of clauses is: a file
written that way carries two indentation schemes, thirteen columns for the
configuration and two for everything else.  A form this common is a fact
about the macro rather than a case for `indent-rules` in every project's
`sift.yaml`, and it belongs in the table.

** No reflowing
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: no-reflow
:END:

sift never moves a line break that the author put somewhere structurally
meaningful, and it never joins two lines that the author split.  It fixes
indentation and spacing on the lines that exist.

*Rationale.*  A line break in Lisp records where the author judged a form to
be getting long.  A pretty-printer that decides the breaks again produces
diffs that are not about the change being made, and diffs of that kind stop
being read.  The same property makes the formatter safe on a file being
edited: no line moves out from under a cursor.

** Reader prefixes are glued to their datum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prefixes
:END:

`'foo`, `` `(a ,b) ``, `#'bar`, `#+sbcl ...` and `#.(...)` are one unit.
The formatter closes any gap between a prefix and its datum, and
`reader-prefix-break` (SIFT007) reports a file that has one.

*Rationale.*  A prefix is not a form; it is part of the next form's name.  A
line break between the two reads as a break at the wrong level, and in Common
Lisp a whitespace-sensitive feature expression turns it into a hazard.

** Comments stay on their line
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: comments
:END:

An inline comment stays on the line it was written on, one space after the
code — or aligned across a run of contiguous lines when `--comment-align`
is on:

#+begin_src scheme
(let ((a 1)    ; the first
      (b 22))  ; and the second
  (+ a b))
#+end_src

A standalone comment keeps its own line and takes the indentation of the
form it is in.

*Rationale.*  A comment's position is part of its meaning: `; the default is
0` on the line of a binding is about that binding.  Moving it edits the
program's documentation, which no formatter should do silently.

** A docstring is written for the tools that render it
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: docstrings
:END:

The first line is a capitalised sentence ending in a period and short
enough to fit in `apropos` (74 columns in Emacs Lisp).  The first sentence
is in the imperative — "Return the value", not "Returns the value".
Continuation lines start at column zero of the string.  In Emacs Lisp the
arguments are named in the docstring; in Guile they are written with
`@var{}` so the manual typesets them as metavariables.

#+begin_src elisp
(defun add (a b)
  "Add A and B.

Return the sum, which is a number."
  (+ a b))
#+end_src

#+begin_src scheme
(define (add a b)
  "Add @var{a} and @var{b}.

Return the sum, which is a number."
  (+ a b))
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  A docstring is the only documentation most functions will ever
have, and the tools that display it — `C-h f`, `apropos`, `(help add)`, the
Guile manual — render it in a fixed way.  A third-person opening reads as a
description of the function rather than as an instruction to its caller, and
an argument that is never mentioned is an argument the reader must infer.

** `when` and `unless` state the test once
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: when-unless
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(if (ready?) (go))              ; => (when (ready?) (go))
(if (not (ready?)) (wait))      ; => (unless (ready?) (wait))
(when (not (ready?)) (wait))    ; => (unless (ready?) (wait))
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  An `if` with one branch is a `when`.  An `if` whose test is
negated requires the reader to perform a negation while reading, and reading
code should not require arithmetic.  Both rewrites are purely syntactic: the
same test is evaluated and the same forms run.  The checks are SIFT012
(`if-without-else`), SIFT033 (`negated-if`), SIFT013 and SIFT014.

** Names carry the type

A Lisp has no type declarations to read, so a name carries most of what a
reader knows of a value: `positive?` is a boolean, `positive` is a number,
`*port*` is special, `+pi+` is a constant.  Four rules keep the conventions of
the four dialects apart:

- *SIFT040* `naming-convention`: a definition name with an underscore
  (`read_file`) or camelCase (`readFile`) is not a Lisp name.  The rule looks
  at definitions only, because a variable bound to somebody else's function is
  not the linter's to rename.
- *SIFT041* `predicate-name`: a procedure whose *last* body form is a
  comparison or another predicate returns a boolean, so its name should end in
  `?` in Scheme and Common Lisp, and in `-p` (or `?`) in Emacs Lisp.  Only the
  last form counts, because that is the value the procedure returns.
- *SIFT042* `special-variable-name`: Common Lisp earmuffs its special
  variables (`*port*`) and plus-quotes its constants (`+pi+`); Emacs Lisp does
  neither, so `*port*` in an Emacs file is a habit from the other dialect.
- None of them has a fix.  Renaming a definition changes every call site in
  every file, and a formatter that renames one of them has broken the program.

** A long expression is bound with `let`
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: let
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
;; SIFT031 suggests binding the repeated part
(define (f x)
  (list (car (cdr (assq 'a (cdr x))))
        (car (cdr (assq 'b (cdr x))))))

(define (f x)
  (let ((rest (cdr x)))
    (list (car (cdr (assq 'a rest)))
          (car (cdr (assq 'b rest))))))
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  `let` is how Lisp names an intermediate result, and a name is
what stops a reader re-deriving it.  Deep nesting (more than four levels) or a
very long body form (more than twelve elements) is the signal that the
expression wants a step.  A form made of clauses is not counted: the elements
of a `cond` or a `loop` are one per clause, not one expression to split, and
`let*` is the right form when a later binding
refers to an earlier one, and `let` when the bindings are independent; a
binding that does not refer to itself belongs in a `let`, which is what
SIFT015 states of a `letrec` with one binding.

The check has no fix: the name and the split are the author's work, and a
formatter that supplied them would be writing the program rather than
formatting it.

** Keyword arguments past five
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: keywords
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(make-window a b c d e f g)                       ; SIFT032
(make-window x y :width 10 :height 20 :title "s") ; says what it means
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  A positional argument is readable only while the reader
remembers the argument list.  That memory holds for five arguments: at the
call site the sixth `#t` states nothing, and a keyword states the parameter's
name.  `sift` reports the call rather than the definition, because the shape
of the callee is the author's to decide; the rule skips special forms and the
calls meant to be variadic (`list`, `format`, `+`), and the limit is
configuration (`max-positional`).

A *record* macro is the other thing that is not a call, and it is the one that
matters most in a Guix file:

#+begin_src scheme
(package                                    ; SIFT032, if it were a call
  (name "hello")
  (version "2.12")
  (build-system gnu-build-system)
  (home-page "https://example.org")
  (synopsis "A package")
  (description "...")
  (license license:gpl3+))
#+end_src

Each argument of `package` is a *field* of the record it builds, so their
number is the size of a package description and not the length of a
parameter list; `(package :name "hello" :version "2.12")` is not Guix, and a
rule that asked for it would be asking for code no Guix command reads.
`package` and `operating-system` are exempt out of the box, and `field-macros`
names the record macros of a project's own DSL -- sift cannot know the name of
a macro it has never seen.

A *declaration* is not a call either, and the two look enough alike to fool a
rule that only counts:

#+begin_src scheme
(use-modules (srfi srfi-1) (srfi srfi-13) (ice-9 format)
             (ice-9 match) (sift records) (sift reader))  ; six libraries, no call

(define-module (demo)
  #:export (one two three four five six seven))           ; seven names, no call
#+end_src

So sift keeps one table of declarations per dialect -- what a file imports,
what it exports, what it implements, what it tells the compiler -- and both
rules that count ask it with a single question.  Everything inside a
declaration is data, the way the inside of a quote is: `#:export (a b c)' is a
list of names to the reader, and it is one to the rules.  The same holds in
the other two Lisps: `(defpackage :demo (:use :cl) (:export #:a ...))` and
`(require \='cl-lib)' are declarations, and `#:export` and `:export` are
clauses whose operator is a keyword rather than a procedure.

A parameter list is the third thing that looks like a call.  Where a form keeps
its parameters is the dialect's business -- `(define (f a) ...)' inside its
second element, `(defun f (a) ...)' in its third -- so sift reads that from a
table too, and a `too-many-arguments` finding counts only what a caller must
pass positionally: everything from the first `&optional`, `&key` or dot
onwards is the caller's choice.

A *pair form* is the fourth: Emacs Lisp states several variables in one
`setq`, `setq-default`, `setq-local` or `setopt`, and Emacs Lisp and Common
Lisp state their places that way in `setf`, `psetf` and `psetq`.  What a
reader counts there is the pairs -- a declaration is two arguments taken in as
one line -- so a `setq` of ten declarations is an ordinary thing to write,
where a call of twenty arguments is not:

#+begin_src elisp
(setq a 1 b 2 c 3 d 4 e 5 f 6 g 7 h 8 i 9)   ; nine pairs, no call
#+end_src

A pair form is judged on its pairs rather than on its argument count: the
limit is twice `max-positional`, which at the default allows ten declarations
in one form.  A `setq` with an argument left without a value is a different
problem, and one SIFT032 does not state -- it counts the pairs it can read and
says nothing about the rest.

** Packages are named, and never reached into
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: packages
:END:

In Common Lisp an exported symbol is written with its package prefix
(`app:run`), and a double colon (`other::internal`) belongs to debugging, not
production.  SIFT025 and SIFT026 enforce this: a use of `other::internal`
breaks when `other` is reorganised, and an unqualified `run` depends on
whatever `*package*` happens to be at read time.

** A file says what it uses
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: imports
:END:

An R7RS file opens by saying which libraries it means, and sift reads that
sentence rather than the names inside the libraries:

#+begin_src scheme
(import (scheme base)
        (only (srfi 1) fold))      ; SIFT060: `car` was here and nothing used it

(import (scheme base) (my lib))    ; SIFT061: no file declares `(my lib)'
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  An import nothing uses is a claim the file has stopped keeping,
and an import of a library that does not exist is a program that will not run.
Both questions are decidable without any knowledge of the library's contents,
which is the line sift holds everywhere else: it reads the text it was given.

Where sift *can* see the contents -- the names an `only' or `rename' clause
spells out, and the `(export ...)' of a library the project declares -- it
reports the unused one by name, and its fix deletes exactly that.  Where it
cannot, it stays silent: a plain `(import (scheme base))' is never reported,
because most of what a standard library offers is used indirectly.  The
`library-not-found' rule says nothing at all in a project that declares no
libraries, so a project whose libraries live outside its tree gets no findings
rather than a hundred.

Guile writes the same sentence as clauses of `define-module', and sift reads
those too -- `#:use-module' is an import, `#:export' is a public surface -- so a
project whose modules are Guile modules is judged like one whose modules are
R7RS libraries:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-module (demo client)
  #:use-module (demo records)   ; SIFT060: nothing it exports is used here
  #:export (run))
#+end_src

A definition is the other half of the same question.  A module that lists what
it exports has said what its public surface is, and another module cannot name
anything that is not on that list -- so a top-level definition that is neither
exported nor mentioned anywhere in its own file is code nothing can reach:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-module (demo)
  #:export (run))

(define (helper) 1)              ; used by `run': kept
(define (run) (helper))
(define (forgotten) 2)           ; SIFT062: nothing can reach it
#+end_src

Both halves of that are required, and either alone would be a guess: without an
`#:export' clause there is no public surface to compare against (a script
exports nothing and is right to), and a name that appears under a quote is a
mention sift cannot rule out as a use, so it counts as one.  The rule says
nothing at all about a file with no `define-module`, which is most Scheme.

The one place sift looks outside the file it was handed is this: the libraries
beside it, read for their `(library ...)' and `(define-library ...)' forms and
for their `define-module' -- the same three ways a file names itself and says
what it exports.  The scan is bounded and cached per directory, because whether
a library exists is a question about the project.

* Riastradh's rules, and which of them sift keeps
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh
:END:

Taylor R. Campbell's *Lisp Style Rules* -- cited as Riastradh's style guide --
is the shortest sharp argument for the choices above.  sift agrees with it
more often than not, and every disagreement is stated as a setting rather than
fixed as an opinion in the program.

** Alignment: the same rule, already implemented
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh-alignment
:END:

Campbell's alignment rule is the one this formatter was built on.  When the
first argument is on the operator's line, everything after it lines up under
that argument; when the operator stands alone, what follows lines up under the
operator; and a list of literal data lines up in one column, whatever its
first element is.

#+begin_src scheme
(+ (sqrt -1)
   (* x y))

(+
 (sqrt -1)
 (* x y))

((car x)
 (cdr x)
 foo)

("foo" "bar" "baz"
 "quux")
#+end_src

The third form is the one editors disagree about: GNU Emacs indents a list
whose operator is itself a list under the first *operand*, and Campbell
argues -- correctly, and sift agrees -- that the operands of a call share a
column whatever the operator looks like, because an operand indented
differently from its siblings no longer reads as a sibling.

That rule is why keyword arguments require no rule of their own: `:width' is
just the second argument, and it aligns where every second argument does.

** Comment levels
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh-comments
:END:

The number of semicolons says what a comment is *for*, before its sentence is
read: four for a heading, three for a comment about the code that follows at
top level, two for a comment inside a form, one for a comment at the end of a
line.

#+begin_src scheme
;;;; Frob Grovel

;;; This section of the code has some important implications.
(define (fnord zarquon)
  ;; If zob, then veeblefitz.
  (quux zot
        mumble                        ;Zibblefrotz.
        frotz))
#+end_src

*SIFT051* (`comment-semicolons`) reports a comment whose level is wrong, and a
comment whose text starts hard against its semicolons -- which Campbell allows
only in a margin comment, where the semicolon is a gesture rather than a
level.  A margin comment is never reported: it belongs to the line of code it
ends, and its level is not a claim about anything.

The rule carries a fix, and it is the mechanical half of the rule.  How many
semicolons a comment takes is decided by the position it occupies, which is
something the reader knows, so ~sift fix~ re-levels every comment it reports --
one at the end of a line, two inside a form, three at top level, a heading of
four left alone -- and puts a space after the semicolons.  The sentence of the
comment is untouched.  Whether the comment is about the right thing, and
whether it is in the right place at all, remains a question the author alone
can answer.

** One blank line, and not one more
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh-blank-lines
:END:

Two blank lines state what one states, at twice the cost, and a blank line in
the middle of a body is a paragraph break inside a paragraph.  Campbell's rule
is one blank line between top-level forms, and none inside a body except to
separate internal definitions.

Half of that is a setting and half of it is a warning, because the two halves
are different jobs:

- `blank-lines: one' makes the formatter *separate adjacent top-level forms
  with exactly one blank line*, inserting one where the author wrote none.  It
  is opt-in, because it is the only thing sift adds to a file; `preserve', the
  default, keeps the author's grouping and only collapses runs that are longer
  than the limit.

  #+begin_src yaml
  blank-lines: one
  #+end_src

- *SIFT052* (`blank-line-in-body`) reports a blank line between two forms of
  one body, and says nothing when the forms on either side are internal
  definitions -- where there really are two paragraphs to separate.

A heading comment belongs to the form below it, so `blank-lines: one' puts the
blank line before the comment as well; a gap that follows a comment is the
author's grouping and is left alone.

** Names
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh-names
:END:

Campbell's naming rules are already the three in [[#rules][the catalogue]]:
hyphenated lower-case words (SIFT040), `?` for a predicate (SIFT041), and
earmuffs and plus-quotes in Common Lisp (SIFT042).  What was missing is the
first rule of the lot: *do not write single-letter variable names*.

*SIFT053* (`single-letter-name`) looks where a reader reads a variable -- the
parameters of a procedure and the names of a binding list -- and reports a
name of one letter.  Index variables are the exception Campbell allows, and
which letters a project reads that way is not something a rule can decide, so
the list is a setting:

#+begin_src yaml
single-letter-names: [i, j, k]
#+end_src

It never looks at a *use*, only at a binding: a use is whatever the binding
said it was, and a definition whose name is one letter names a program rather
than a variable.

** What sift does not take from the guide
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: riastradh-not
:END:

- *Line length of 72 or 80.*  The limit is a setting (`line-length`), and the
  default is 96 for Scheme, Racket and Common Lisp, because a modern screen
  holds two such files side by side.  A project that wants Campbell's 80
  columns sets `line-length: 80`; Emacs Lisp already gets 80.
- *Pages and form feeds.*  Paging a file rewrites the line number of every
  line below the break, so a formatter that paginates makes every diff in a
  version-controlled file a diff about pagination.  The line numbers in a
  diagnostic, a patch and a `git blame` all depend on the file staying the
  shape it is.
- *Top-level forms of at most twenty-one lines.*  That is a judgement about
  how a program is split into procedures, and it is the author's.  SIFT031
  says when a form is too deep or too long to read at once, which is the part
  a tool can see.
- *`with-' and `call-with-' prefixes.*  Telling those two apart needs to know
  whether a procedure argument is *called* and what it means for dynamic
  state, which is a guess about a program's intent rather than a fact about
  its text.

* The rule catalogue
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: rules
:END:

Every rule carries a name (the string a configuration uses) and a code (the
token an editor shows and a bug report quotes).  The code is stable: it is
never reused, and a new rule takes the next number.

The complete, generated list — every rule with its code, dialects, severity,
example and fix — is in [[file:rules.org][rules.org]], written by ~maak docs~ from the rule
records themselves, so it cannot name a rule that does not exist and the test
suite fails when it falls behind.  What follows is the reasoning: the rules
grouped by dialect, with the case for each one.

These rules are cross-dialect because they read the *text* (whitespace, length,
delimiters) or a form all four dialects share (`if`, `when`, `progn`, `let`).
Bookkeeping specific to one dialect is in that dialect's section.

*SIFT003 at 96 columns:* the limit is soft -- it warns, and the line is not
reflowed.  Scheme, Racket and Common Lisp get 96; Emacs Lisp gets 80, the width
the Emacs manual assumes for a docstring's first line and for two source files
read side by side.  The number is a default rather than a law: `--line-length`,
or `line-length:` in a config file, moves it for a project that writes to a
different width.

*The case for SIFT001 and SIFT002:* trailing whitespace is invisible and
appears in every diff; a tab renders at a different width in every editor, so
indentation written with one is not indentation the next reader will see.

*SIFT004 has no fix:* placing a `)` where it is supposed to belong is a guess
at what the program means.  sift reports the opening delimiter and stops.

*The case for SIFT006:* `(define (f) (progn (a) (b)))` says "a progn" twice —
the body of a definition is already a sequence.

*SIFT051, SIFT052 and SIFT053* are the three layout rules borrowed from
[[#riastradh][Riastradh's rules]]: a comment's number of semicolons, a blank
line in the middle of a body, and a variable whose name is one letter.  All
three are `info`: they are about how a file reads rather than about what it
means, and a project that has decided otherwise excludes them by name.

** Scheme rules

| Code    | Rule                          | Severity | What it says                                             |
|---------+-------------------------------+----------+----------------------------------------------------------|
| SIFT011 | internal-define               | warning  | `define` in a procedure body with no enclosing `let`      |
| SIFT012 | if-without-else               | warning  | `if` with no else branch; `when` says it once             |
| SIFT013 | when-without-body             | warning  | `when` with no body                                       |
| SIFT014 | unless-without-body           | warning  | `unless` with no body                                     |
| SIFT015 | letrec-single-binding         | info     | `letrec` with one non-recursive binding; `let` is enough   |
| SIFT016 | quasiquote-unquote-constant   | info     | a constant is unquoted inside a quasiquote                |
| SIFT035 | docstring-texinfo             | info     | a Guile docstring does not document a parameter with `@var{}` |
| SIFT036 | clause-brackets               | warning  | a `cond` clause is written with the other kind of bracket |
| SIFT070 | letrec-init-reference         | warning  | a `letrec` initializer refers to a binding not yet assigned |
| SIFT093 | quasiquote-dotted-unquote     | warning  | `(key . ,value)` in a quasiquote says the same as `(key unquote value)` |

*SIFT011*: Scheme allows definitions at the head of a body, but a `define`
in the middle of an expression defines in the wrong scope — it is almost
always a `let` that got written as a `define`.

*SIFT013/SIFT014*: `(when x)` does nothing at all.  Either the body was
lost in an edit, or the form should not be there.

*SIFT015*: `letrec` promises that the bindings may refer to each other.
With one binding that does not, it promises something untrue, and the
reader looks for the recursion that is not there.

*SIFT016*: `` `(1 ,2) `` evaluates to the same list as `` `(1 2) ``.  The
unquote is a leftover from a version where the value was computed.

*SIFT093*: a dotted pair whose tail is an unquote is a list of three
elements, so `` `((key . ,value)) `` and `` `((key unquote value)) `` are the
same datum.  The dot reads as two halves and puts the whole rest in the
second one; writing `unquote` out reads as the three elements the reader
builds, and the fix replaces the dot and the comma with the symbol.

*SIFT035*: Guile renders docstrings as Texinfo.  An argument written `x` is
plain text; `@var{x}` is typeset as a metavariable in `C-h f` and in the
manual, which is what a parameter is.

*SIFT036*: see [[#brackets][Square brackets for `cond` clauses]].

** Guix rules

The Guix manual's packaging chapter and the manual's own G-Expressions
chapter are another style document a Scheme file can be held to, and
sift reads them as Scheme: a `.scm` package definition is a file of forms,
`#~(...)` is a reader prefix on a datum, and a build phase is a lambda.  Four
of these rules concern the gexp seam itself, three concern what a gexp states
where another form would state it more plainly, and four concern the package
record's own vocabulary.

| Code    | Rule                        | Severity | What it says                                             |
|---------+-----------------------------+----------+----------------------------------------------------------|
| SIFT077 | gexp-quoted                 | warning  | a gexp written under a quote, where what is quoted is the form |
| SIFT078 | ungexp-outside-gexp         | warning  | `#$`, `#$@`, `#+` or `#+@` with no gexp around it        |
| SIFT079 | gexp-output-script          | warning  | `#$output` in a gexp that makes a file rather than a derivation |
| SIFT080 | assoc-ref-outputs           | warning  | `(assoc-ref %outputs "out")` inside a gexp, where `#$output` says it |
| SIFT081 | string-append-file-append   | info     | a gexp `string-append` of a package and a literal path    |
| SIFT082 | phase-name-quoting          | warning  | a phase named by an unquoted symbol or by a string        |
| SIFT083 | use-modules-in-gexp         | warning  | a build module imported inside a gexp nothing imported it into |
| SIFT084 | package-synopsis            | warning  | a synopsis with an article, a trailing period, or no capital |
| SIFT085 | package-description-literal | warning  | a description built rather than written as one string     |
| SIFT086 | sha256-base32               | warning  | a `sha256` that is not `(base32 "...")` of 52 characters |
| SIFT087 | inputs-label-alist          | warning  | an input list written as a labelled alist                  |

The gexp rules are written on the reader's shape rather than on a guess
about evaluation, which is what makes them decidable.  *SIFT077* is the
manual's own sentence -- "a quoted gexp is not evaluated" -- turned into a
finding: `'#~(...)` quotes the form, so the build system receives a list
beginning with `gexp` and does nothing with it.  *SIFT078* is the other side
of the same seam: `#$` outside a gexp is a token no form defines, almost
always a gexp that lost its `#~` in an edit.  *SIFT079* is the manual's
caveat about `#$output` -- it "only makes sense for gexps passed to
`gexp->derivation`" -- and `program-file`, `scheme-file`, `gexp->script`
and `gexp->file` all make a store *file*, where `getenv "out"` answers
false.

*SIFT080* and *SIFT081* are the two places where Guix code written before
g-exps still works and no longer says what the manual says.  `%outputs` was
the alist a build read its output directory out of; inside a gexp the
directory is `#$output`, and the fix writes it.  `(string-append #$coreutils
"/bin/ls")` builds a file name at run time from the package's directory,
where `#$(file-append coreutils "/bin/ls")` puts the whole absolute file
name into the store path, so `grep` and the closure can see the file the
script will run.  The fix is a rewrite of the whole call, `#$` included,
because the ungexp is inside the form being replaced.

*SIFT082* is `.dir-locals.el`: a `modify-phases` clause names its phase with
a symbol, and `(delete configure)` deletes the value of a variable instead.
*SIFT083* is the scoping rule the manual explains at length: `use-modules`
inside a gexp is read by the builder, where only the modules
`with-imported-modules` marked exist, and the marking is lexical -- a gexp
returned from a procedure called inside it is not marked.

The last four are the package record's own vocabulary.  *SIFT084* is the
manual's three sentences about a synopsis: capitalised, no trailing period,
and not opening with an article "which usually does not bring anything".
*SIFT085* is why: xgettext extracts synopses and descriptions, and it can
only extract a literal string -- `string-append` and `format` make a string
the translator never sees.  *SIFT086* is the encoding: a `sha256` is
`(base32 "...")`, and 32 bytes in base32 is 52 lower-case characters, so a
shorter string cannot be the hash the download has.  *SIFT087* is the pre-gexp
labels: `(("gawk" ,gawk))` names each package twice, and an input list is a
list of packages.

** Racket rules, and the ones that stay out of a Racket file
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: racket
:END:

Racket is the fourth dialect, and adding it was not a matter of another row in
a table of extensions: every rule it runs on had to answer whether its premise
still held.  A rule that is wrong for the language it is run on teaches a
reader to ignore the linter, so the ones that do not hold are left out, and
here is why each of them is.

The rules a Racket file gets are the ones whose subject is a form every Lisp
shares -- `cond`, `case`, `let`, `apply`, `quote`, a binding list, a body --
plus the twenty that read *text* and therefore never had a dialect at all:
whitespace, line length, comment levels, delimiter balance, naming, nesting.
That is forty-six of the ninety-three.

Six rules do not apply, and each for a reason a reader can check:

- *SIFT060* `unused-import`, *SIFT061* `library-not-found` and *SIFT062*
  `unused-definition` read `import`, `define-library` and `define-module`.
  Racket's `require` and `provide` are a different grammar -- `only-in`,
  `except-in`, `prefix-in`, `rename-in`, and a `provide` that can re-export a
  contract -- and reading them with the Scheme one would report a file in the
  vocabulary of a language it is not written in.  The next rule to write here
  is Racket's own.
- *SIFT088* `call-arity` reads a parameter list whose optionals and keywords are
  spelled `&optional` and `#:key`.  Racket spells an optional parameter
  `[name default]`, which the arity reader counts as required, so the rule
  would report `(define (f a [b 1]) ...)` and every call of it.
- *SIFT069* `case-literal-datum` exists because Scheme's `case` compares with
  `eqv?`, which cannot decide a string.  Racket's `case` compares with
  `equal?`, so a string datum in a Racket `case` matches, and the rule's
  premise -- not its implementation -- is what is false there.
- *SIFT033* `negated-if` repairs `(if (not x) a)` into `(unless x a)`.  Racket's
  `if` has two arms and no others, so that rewrite loses a branch.  The `when`
  half of the rule is correct and travels with it, and a rule cannot be half
  right.
- *SIFT035* `docstring-texinfo` reads the string a Guile `define` keeps as
  documentation.  A Racket `define` has no such slot: its first string is an
  ordinary body expression, and the documentation convention is Scribble
  `@racket[x]`, which is not `@var{x}`.
- *SIFT006* `redundant-progn` is about `progn`, which Racket spells `begin`.
  The rule is silent there rather than wrong, and the Racket spelling would be
  a rule of its own.

Three more of the dialect's own conventions are honoured rather than imposed:

- A top-level comment takes *two* semicolons in Racket, not three.  *SIFT051*
  asks for two there and still accepts three and four as the headings they are,
  which is the level `raco fmt` keeps and the one a Racket style guide shows.
- A `cond`, `case` or `match` clause is written with a square bracket, as in
  Scheme, and *SIFT036* enforces the same choice for both.
- A keyword is spelled `#:height`, so the advice *SIFT032* gives a long call
  says `#:key` in a Racket buffer, and a signature in the language server
  spells the keyword the way the language does.

Two limits are worth stating beside the rules.  A `#lang` line is kept whole
and is never reformatted, because it is the header of the file rather than a
form in it.  And a `match` pattern is a pattern: no rule reads `(list a b)` in
`(match x [(list a b) ...])` as a call to `list`, which is what six rules would
otherwise have done to every `match` in the file.

One suggestion needs a caveat in Racket: *SIFT047* `composition` proposes
`(compose f g)` for a nested lambda, and `compose` lives in
`racket/function`, so the fix is worth making only in a file that requires it.
The same rule has the same caveat in Guile, where `compose` comes from
`(srfi srfi-1)`.

** Rules that read the whole file

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: inference
:END:

Five rules cannot be answered by looking at one form, and each stops where the
source stops saying: a call's arity is a fact about the definition it resolves
to, a literal's type is a fact about the literal, a comparison's predicate is a
fact about what it compares against, and an import is unused only once the rest
of the file has been read.

| Code    | Rule                      | Severity | What it says                                           |
|---------+---------------------------+----------+--------------------------------------------------------|
| SIFT088 | call-arity                | info     | a call whose arguments do not match the definition in this file |
| SIFT089 | string-literal-argument   | warning  | a number or boolean where the operator's convention says a string |
| SIFT090 | number-literal-argument   | warning  | a string passed to an operator whose arguments are numbers |
| SIFT091 | unused-import-from        | info     | a `:import-from` name the file never uses              |
| SIFT092 | eq-non-symbol             | warning  | `eq`/`eq?` of a literal that identity cannot decide     |

*Reading, not inference.*  A type system would answer all five and cost a
compiler, and a language server that guessed would be wrong in the one place a
reader trusts it.  Every one of these rules reads the source itself: `+`
takes numbers because that is what the operator is, `string-append` takes
strings because that is what its name says, `1` is a number, `"1"` is a
string, `(eq? x 1)` cannot decide two numbers that were read separately, and a
name imported into a package and never mentioned is a name nobody used.  No
variable is typed by any of them: `(string-append "a" b)` says nothing about
`b`, and sift says nothing either.

*The pass order.*  Each of these needs the file's definition table, which
means the whole buffer has been read and, for `call-arity`, walked.  That is
fast -- milliseconds on a file of a few thousand lines -- but it is not free,
so the language server publishes the findings that need only the form in front
of it first and these ones second, from a thread of its own, as a second report
for the same buffer version.  The editor draws the first immediately and
replaces it when the second arrives; a keystroke never waits for the second
pass.  `lint` on the command line runs all five with the rest, because a
command that is already reading the whole file has the whole file.

** What the three communities' guides ask for
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: guides
:END:

A survey of the documents each community writes for itself -- Riastradh's
*Lisp Style Rules* and the R7RS report for Scheme, the Google Common Lisp
style guide and Mallet's rule catalogue for Common Lisp, the Emacs Lisp
Reference Manual's Coding Conventions together with `checkdoc', `package-lint'
and `elisp-lint` for Emacs Lisp -- yields the shapes below, each of which
concerns the text rather than what the author meant.

Three are simplifications the guides state as equivalences, so they carry
fixes:

#+begin_src scheme
(apply f 1 (list 2 3))               ; SIFT064: this is (f 1 2 3)
(f 1 2 3)

(let ((a (f)) (b (g a))) b)         ; SIFT065: `a' does not exist yet
(let* ((a (f)) (b (g a))) b)

(let* ((a (f)) (b (g))) (+ a b))    ; SIFT066: nothing depends on order
(let ((a (f)) (b (g))) (+ a b))

(append xs '())                     ; SIFT068: the empty list adds nothing
xs
#+end_src

Two are bugs the guides name outright.  *SIFT067* is the Common Lisp and
Emacs Lisp half of "`nil' is both the empty list and false": `(eq x nil)` asks
whether two objects are the same one, and `null` is the predicate that says
what the code means.  *SIFT069* is Guile's own `bad-case-datum` warning by
another name: a `case` clause is compared with `eqv?`, which cannot decide a
string, so `(case x (("a") 1))` is a clause that can never match.

*SIFT070* is R7RS's rule for `letrec`: an initializer may not refer to the
value of any variable the form is binding, because none of them has been
assigned when it runs.  All-lambda initializers satisfy that condition
automatically, which is why `letrec` normally holds a set of mutually
recursive procedures; another initializer that mentions a binding is a
`letrec*' under the wrong name.

The Guix manual is the fourth of these documents, and the one a Scheme
packager is held to besides the language: its packaging chapter supplies
{ SIFT084, SIFT085, SIFT086, SIFT087 } and its G-Expressions chapter supplies
{ SIFT077, SIFT078, SIFT079, SIFT080, SIFT081, SIFT082, SIFT083 }.  They are
[[#rules][the Guix rules]], and the reason they are decidable is the reason
all of these are: each names a shape -- a quoted gexp, a phase named by a
variable, a description that is a call rather than a string -- rather than a
belief about what the build does.

The last six are dialect bookkeeping.  *SIFT071* and *SIFT073* are the Google
guide's package and lambda-list rules: `:use` hides where a name came from and
`&optional` before `&key` makes the first keyword a caller writes an optional
argument.  *SIFT072* is Mallet's `wrong-otherwise`: `ecase` exists to be
exhaustive, and a default clause is the one thing that stops it being checked.
*SIFT074* is the Emacs Lisp manual's deprecation of `cl` in favour of
`cl-lib`.  *SIFT075* is the warning the byte compiler already emits --
`save-excursion` saves point for one buffer, and a `set-buffer` inside it
changes which buffer `point` means.  *SIFT076* is the same idea read the other
way: a command with required parameters whose `(interactive)` supplies none
cannot be called interactively at all.

** The same meaning in fewer parentheses

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: simplification
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(lambda (x) (f x))            ; SIFT045: this is `f'
f

((lambda (x) (f x)) (g))      ; SIFT046: this is a `let'
(let ((x (g))) (f x))

(lambda (x) (f (g x)))        ; SIFT047: this is a pipeline
(compose f g)

(let ((x (f))) x)             ; SIFT048: a binding that exists to be returned
(f)
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  These four shapes are syntactic equivalences rather than matters
of taste: each of them *is* the shorter form.  A lambda that passes its
argument straight on is the procedure; a lambda applied where it is written is
a `let`; a lambda whose body is one call around another is a composition; a
`let` that binds a name only to return it is its value, and a `let` with no
bindings is its body.

The rules are the shape, not the style, and every condition that would make
the rewrite wrong is checked first: one plain formal, exactly that formal
passed, an operator that is a procedure rather than a special form or a macro,
no rest argument, no keywords.  `(lambda (x) (if x))` is not reported because
`if` is not a value, and `(lambda (x) (f x y))` is not reported because the
call says something the lambda does not.  SIFT045 and SIFT047 are Scheme only:
`(lambda (x) (f x))` is not a function designator in Common Lisp, and `compose`
is Guile's.

All four have fixes, because each one is a syntactic equivalence and nothing
has to be named.  A fix is emitted only where the equivalence can be stated
with that confidence.

** SXML is markup, and markup has rules

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sxml
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
(div (p "a") (@ (class "x")))              ; SIFT049: attributes come first
(div (@ (class "x")) (p "a"))

(div (@) (p "a"))                          ; SIFT049: and there is one of them
(div (p "a"))

(ul (@ (id "x")) ((li "a") (li "b")))      ; SIFT050: a child is a child
(ul (@ (id "x")) (li "a") (li "b"))

(div (@ (class "card")))                   ; SIFT063: an element with no children
(div (@ (class "card")) "")
#+end_src

*Rationale.*  SXML is Lisp used as markup — `(element (@ (attribute "value"))
child ...)` — and a formatter needs no knowledge of that: an element is a
list, and the attribute list, being the first argument, aligns under itself as
in every call.  A linter can state the three things the shape cannot: the
attribute list comes first and occurs once, a child is an element, a string or
a number rather than a list of them, and an element with no children at all is
an element whose tag will not be closed.

What makes these rules safe to state is the test for markup: the attribute
list itself.  A list whose head is a symbol and that holds an `(@ ...)` child
is an element, and nothing else is judged.  `(f "a" "b")` in ordinary code is
a call with two strings, and SXML says nothing about it.

*The severity of SIFT063:* `(div (@ (class "card")))` serializes as
`<div class="card"/>`.  XML reads that as a complete element; HTML does not —
the self-closing form exists only for the void elements, so a browser keeps the
`div` open and every following element ends up inside it.  One omitted child
is therefore a page whose layout collapses, with nothing in the source to show
for it.  The element that really is empty says so: `(div (@ (class "card"))
"")` is an empty text child, and every serializer writes it as `<div
class="card"></div>`.

SIFT063 has a fix, because the edit is the one the author would make by hand
and it adds nothing to the document: the element's own text with ` ""` before
its closing delimiter.  Writing the child of an element that *is* meant to be
self-closing — an HTML `img`, `br` or `input`, or any element of a document
that is XML rather than HTML — is the author's call, so those say
`; sift:disable-next-line sxml-empty-element`, or the project leaves the rule
off with `exclude-checkers: sxml-empty-element` in `sift.yaml`.  The two shape
rules have no fix: moving an attribute list is a rearrangement of markup, and
the missing element name in `((li "a") (li "b"))` is the author's to supply —
a formatter that invented `div` there would be guessing at the document.

** Three rules about shape

*SIFT008* (`empty-form`) exists because `()` reads as "the empty list" and
evaluates as an error in Scheme: it is data that needs a quote, and the place
it happens is almost always a forgotten `'()`.  The rule is careful not to
fire on a *parameter* list, a binding list, the literals of a `syntax-rules` or
`syntax-case` transformer, or quoted data — where `()` is exactly right — and
it says nothing at all in Emacs Lisp and Common Lisp, where `()` is `nil`.

*SIFT037* (`duplicate-binding`) catches `(let ((x 1) (x 2)) ...)`.  In `let*`
it is legal and still almost always a mistake: the first binding is dead, and
a reader cannot see that without comparing names.

*SIFT009* (`duplicate-keyword`) catches `(make-window :width 10 :width 20)`:
one of the two values is being discarded, and nothing in the code says which.
A form made of clauses is not a call with keyword arguments and is not
checked: every keyword of a `loop` starts a clause, so `:of-type` written
twice there declares the type of two different variables, and two `:do`
clauses both run.

*SIFT038* (`docstring-before-interactive`) is the Emacs Lisp rule that is
easiest to get wrong by accident: Emacs reads a docstring from the *first*
form of the body, so a string after `(interactive)` documents nothing and
`C-h f` shows nothing, while the file looks documented.

* What sift will not do
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: limits
:END:

- *It will not reflow.*  Long lines are reported (SIFT003), not broken.
- *It will not guess.*  An unbalanced file is refused, with the position of
  the opening delimiter, unless `--force` directs it to format what it can
  read.
- *It will not fix without `--fix`,* and it will not apply a fix that makes
  the file parse worse.  A fix that would introduce a new parse error is
  discarded along with every other fix of that run.
- *It will not rewrite an expression.*  Every fix is either a delimiter, a
  piece of whitespace, a docstring insertion, or a syntactic equivalence
  (`if (not x)` → `unless x`).  Names are never invented.
- *It will not touch a datum comment, a `#.` form or a `#<…>` object.*  Those
  are copied verbatim; formatting inside them would be a guess about code
  the reader was told not to read.

* How to disagree
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: disagreeing
:END:

1. *Turn a rule off* for a line or a form:

   #+begin_src scheme
   ;; sift:disable-next-line keyword-arguments
   (make-window a b c d e f g)

   ;; sift:disable wrap-in-let
   (define (f x)
     ...)
   ;; sift:enable wrap-in-let
   #+end_src

2. *Run fewer rules:* `--checker`, `--exclude-checker`, or a `checkers:`
   list in `sift.yaml`.

3. *Change a threshold:* `line-length`, `max-depth`, `max-elements`,
   `max-positional`, `indent`, `brackets`, `blank-lines`, `loop-keywords`,
   `single-letter-names`.

4. *Ask why:* `sift explain RULE` prints the rule's code, description,
   dialects, severity, a failing example and the fix it expects; and
   `M-x sift-explain-at-point` in Emacs does it at the line at point.

* The style in one page
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: summary
:END:

#+begin_src scheme
;;; Commentary:
;;
;; Two columns, one blank line, closers that trail.
;;; Code:

(define (classify n)
  "Return a symbol describing @var{n}.

The name says more than the number does at the call site."
  (cond [(< n 0) 'negative]
        [(= n 0) 'zero]
        [else 'positive]))

(define (report n)
  (let ((kind (classify n)))   ; the name is the documentation
    (when (symbol? kind)       ; one branch: when, not if
      (display kind))))
#+end_src

Everything above is what `sift format` produces from the same code written
without care, and everything that is not whitespace was already the
author's.
